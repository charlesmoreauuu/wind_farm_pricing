Is fixing the 24-year-old wind farm cheaper than building a new one?

The ACCRAR wind farm has 50 turbines, 700 MW of capacity, and sits 25 km off the east coast of Scotland. It is in year 24 of a 27-year operational design life. Rambøll wants to know whether it makes economic sense to keep it running for another 10 years. This tool runs the numbers.

Repair costs a small fraction of new-build capex. The asset earns the money back in under a year. Electricity from the extended life costs roughly a third of new-build LCOE. We picked 10 years because Rambøll's Meeting 3 verdict was that 15 is too long for the welds to survive. The team's FLS shows 10 years stays inside the fatigue allowance.

Why the totals may be conservative

Rambøll Meeting 3 specifically advised: "Combine activities where possible." This catalogue prices each measure as a standalone activity (separate IRATA crew mobilisations, separate vessel transits). In practice the secondary-steel campaign and the coatings campaign would share crews, vessels, mobilisation cost and weather windows — a realistic bundling discount on labour and mobilisation is ~5–10%, which the current totals do not apply. If you want to model bundling, reduce the campaign labour line by that percentage and re-run.

Is the extended-life electricity cheaper than building new?

LCOE? is how the industry compares the cost of different power sources on equal terms. For new offshore wind in the UK, it lands between £55 and £124 per MWh ( Arup 2024 benchmark? ). Ours comes out around £32 because we skip the giant upfront capex. The asset is already built.

Why is the LCOE so much lower for extension?

Because the expensive bits are already paid for. New offshore wind capex is £3,976/kW. Most of that is predevelopment? (£216/kW), construction? (£2,823/kW), and grid infrastructure? (£937/kW). ACCRAR has paid all of that already. We only need to spend about £…/kW on targeted repairs, roughly 0.3% of new-build capex. The existing grid connection, seabed lease, and OFTO infrastructure all stay in place.

Option D: where the £… M extra goes

Option D adds full major-component refurbishment on top of Option C: 50 × gearboxes @ £750 k = £37.5 M, 50 × generators @ £400 k = £20 M, 50 × transformers @ £200 k = £10 M, 150 × blade refurbishment (leading-edge erosion + lightning-protection renewal) @ £150 k = £22.5 M, SCADA modernisation £5 M. This is justifiable only if the major mechanical components turn out to be near end-of-life on inspection, which today's condition assessments do not indicate. Rambøll Meeting 3 explicitly noted this trade-off.

Carbon-aware design choice already taken

Report §10.4 documents an explicit carbon-driven design decision: an artificial-reef-block alternative for ~10 % of the armour layer (1,870 m³ of ARC Marine units) was assessed and rejected on transport-carbon grounds — manufactured in Torquay, Devon, the 700+ mile haul to Northern Scotland made it carbon- and cost-unfavourable vs. local rock from Findon quarry. This decision is consistent with the broader carbon argument for Option C over Option E.

What if our key assumptions are wrong?

One-variable-at-a-time NPV sensitivity. Each bar shows project NPV when that single variable is swept ±20% from its base value, with everything else held constant. Longer bars = the assumption that matters most to the conclusion.

How to read this

The axis line is the base-case NPV (currently £… M). The red bar shows the NPV at the unfavourable sensitivity (–20%), the green bar shows the favourable case (+20%). For CAPEX the directions flip (less CAPEX is favourable). The longest bars dominate the result and should receive the most attention in your final report. If the red bar never crosses zero, the conclusion is robust to that assumption.
